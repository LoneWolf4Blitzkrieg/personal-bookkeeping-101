# Personal Bookkeeping 101: Course Design

Author: Raul Bautista. Design written October 24, 2026.
Governing standard: `40_Hour_Course_Proposal_v3_phonics.txt` (the "proposal"), in this folder. Where this document is silent, the proposal applies.

Decisions are marked **[Raul Bautista]** (made by the author in conversation) or **[Claude's call]** (proposed by Claude, open to change). Unmarked text restates the proposal.

---

## 1. Intent

**Who it is for. [Raul Bautista]** People just out of high school who are starting to earn their own money. They come at two speeds: slower learners who reread a lesson several times before it makes sense, and fast readers who move through courses quicker than average. Many are frightened of money, spend impulsively, and do not know where their paycheck goes once it arrives.

**What it gives them. [Raul Bautista]** The ability to follow every dollar: keep personal books by hand or on screen, budget by logic rather than emotion, plan for the future, and build an emergency fund. Success is a learner who can say, with their own ledger in front of them, exactly where last month's money went and what next month's will do.

**Tone. [Claude's call]** Calm and free of shame. The course is partly an antidote to fear: each balanced page is evidence that money is understandable. No lectures about past spending; the ledger simply shows what happened.

**Method. [Raul Bautista]** The proposal's systematic phonics model: the code of bookkeeping (accounts, debits, credits, balances) taught explicitly, parts before wholes, in a fixed sequence, with decodable examples only (nothing in an example that has not been taught), practised to automaticity, with mastery gates, spacing, retrieval and no guessing crutches.

## 2. Goals, non-goals, anti-goals

**Goals**
- A complete, self-contained course a learner can take alone in a web browser.
- Main course: bookkeeping, logical budgeting, future planning, emergency fund **[Raul Bautista]**.
- Add-on: the Federal Reserve, IRAs, and the causes of inflation, including corporate pricing **[Raul Bautista]**.
- Publicly available, free to use, with credit to the author **[Raul Bautista]**.

**Non-goals**
- Not investment, tax or legal advice. IRAs and investing are taught as how they work, never as what to buy **[Claude's call, agreed in discussion]**.
- Not business bookkeeping, accounting software training or tax filing.
- No accounts, logins, server or tracking of learners **[Claude's call]**.

**Anti-goals (failure even if it technically works)**
- Any example that is not a double-entry ledger page **[Raul Bautista]**.
- Any real-world figure that is wrong or unsourced **[Raul Bautista]**.
- Debated economic claims presented as settled fact in graded questions (see section 5).
- Copying Coursera's or anyone else's design, text, images or code **[Raul Bautista]**.
- A tone that shames or scares the learner.
- Arithmetic errors in any ledger: every example must balance.

## 3. The ledger rule [Raul Bautista]

Every example in the course is a page of the classic double-entry ledger, modelled on the green ledger sheet the author supplied. Its columns, left to right:

| Column | Meaning in this course |
|---|---|
| Date | Date of the transaction |
| Items | Short description |
| Folio | Page reference to the journal or other ledger page |
| ✓ | Posted mark for the debit |
| Debits | Debit amount |
| ✓ | Posted mark for the credit |
| Credits | Credit amount |
| Dr/Cr | Whether the running balance is a debit or credit balance |
| Balance | Running balance |

Each page also has a header: Sheet No., Account No., Name, and Account (title). The sheet's "Terms, Rating, Credit Limit" header lines are kept visually and used where they make sense (Credit Limit on a credit-card page).

The **journal** (book of original entry) is also shown as a ledger-style page with Date, Items (accounts and explanation), Folio, Debits and Credits columns. **[Claude's call]** It counts as a ledger page for the purposes of the rule.

**Source documents [Raul Bautista].** Documents that get recorded on the ledger may appear beside it when necessary: for example a fictional pay stub from a fictional fast-food restaurant or car wash, handwritten checks, receipts, and a bank statement for reconciliation. They are always shown as the thing being recorded; the teaching happens on the ledger page.

Reports (income statement, net-worth statement, trial balance) are drawn on the same green ruled paper with the same money columns. **[Claude's call]** This keeps "ledger only" true while allowing the reports bookkeeping needs.

Paper or digital **[Raul Bautista]**: the learner chooses. Digital means typing directly into the ledger sheets on the course page; paper means printing blank sheets from the course and writing by hand. Both use identical pages. **[Claude's call]** Gates, checkpoints and the final exam are always answered on screen, so they can be marked and unlock the next module; paper is for lessons, practice and the learner's own books.

**Before debit and credit are taught [Claude's call].** Modules 1 and 2 use the full sheet, but only the Date, Items and Balance columns are active; the other columns are shown greyed with the label "you'll learn this in Module 3". Amounts are written as "money in" and "money out" beside the sheet. This keeps every example a real ledger page while containing nothing untaught, the way a phonics program introduces a few sight words before the full code.

**How the five buckets work [Claude's call].** Buckets are earmarked portions of the learner's money, each with its own asset ledger page (Spending, Saving, Investing, Charity, Blow money), whatever bank accounts actually hold the money. A purchase debits an expense account and credits the bucket it came from. Bank reconciliation (module 10) proves that the bucket pages held at one bank add up to that bank's statement. The Investing bucket simply accumulates until the add-on explains where it can go. The course suggests a starting split of **Spending 60%, Saving 15%, Investing 10%, Charity 5%, Blow money 10%**; each learner may change it, and gates use the split stated in each item. The emergency fund (module 13) and sinking funds (module 14) are named sub-pages of the Saving bucket.

## 4. Course structure

The proposal's template: 16 modules of 2.5 hours (1 hour instruction, 90 minutes practice), about two a week over eight weeks, mastery gate at the end of every module, cumulative checkpoints after modules 4, 8 and 12, final project in modules 15 and 16. The main course may run past 40 hours if it needs to, up to 80 hours in all **[Raul Bautista, cap of 80 hours set October 24, 2026]**; this overrides the proposal's rule of cutting scope instead.

Module outline **[Claude's call; content topics are Raul Bautista's]**. Each teaching module introduces three ideas (42 in all, exactly the proposal's cap; this is why the extension allowance matters):

| # | Module | New ideas |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What money is | what money does (exchange, saving, measuring); dividing every paycheck into five buckets (spending, saving, investing, charity, personal "blow money") before spending; one ledger page per bucket |
| 2 | The ledger page | the columns; the running balance; carrying a balance forward to a new sheet |
| 3 | Debit and credit | the personal accounting equation (what you own = what you owe + what you are worth); debit and credit rules by account type (asset, liability, income, expense, net worth); every entry has equal debit and credit sides |
| 4 | Your paycheck | gross pay versus take-home pay; deductions (federal and state withholding, Social Security, Medicare, other); recording a paycheck across accounts |
| — | Checkpoint A | |
| 5 | Your chart of accounts | personal accounts (cash, checking, savings, credit card, income, expenses, net worth); account numbers; the Folio column as a cross-reference |
| 6 | The journal and posting | writing a journal entry; posting it to ledger pages; the ✓ columns as proof of posting |
| 7 | Everyday spending | receipts as source documents; recording purchases by cash and debit card; transfers between your own accounts and buckets |
| 8 | Credit cards and debt | a credit card as a liability (credit balance); recording charges, payments and interest; the cost of minimum payments |
| — | Checkpoint B | |
| 9 | Proving your books | the trial balance; common errors (transposition, slide, one-sided entry); finding an error by its size |
| 10 | Matching the bank | bank reconciliation; outstanding deposits and payments; entries for bank fees and interest |
| 11 | Budgeting with logic | a budget as a planned ledger; budget versus actual; spending rules decided in advance (needs versus wants, a waiting period before unplanned purchases) |
| 12 | Month-end | closing income and expense accounts to net worth; the monthly income statement; the net-worth statement |
| — | Checkpoint C | |
| 13 | The emergency fund | essential monthly expenses; sizing the fund (the commonly cited three to six months of essential expenses); rules for using and refilling it |
| 14 | Planning ahead | inflation and buying power (cash in a safe versus a savings account earning interest); compound interest; sinking funds for known future costs |
| 15 | Final project I | plan one full month of books |
| 16 | Final project II | keep, prove, reconcile and report the month |

**Add-on [Raul Bautista]**: modules after module 16, graded, outside the main course. **[Claude's call]** Three modules of 2.5 hours, following the full proposal rules (two or three new ideas each, decodable examples, gates with four versions). They are required, since the final exam includes them:
- **A1. The Federal Reserve:** what the Federal Reserve is (created by the Federal Reserve Act of 1913); how money moves among banks, the government and the Fed, shown as simplified ledger pages; how the Fed changes the amount of money.
- **A2. Inflation and its causes:** how inflation is measured (Consumer Price Index); causes economists broadly accept (money supply growth, government deficit spending, rising costs); corporate pricing, taught with company ledgers and the evidence on each side (section 5).
- **A3. IRAs and saving for later:** what an IRA is; traditional versus Roth; contribution limits and compound growth over decades on ledger pages. How they work, not advice.

**Final exam [Raul Bautista]**: taken after A3. At least 80% of its questions and score cover the main course; no more than 20% cover the add-on. **[Raul Bautista, October 24, 2026]** 50 questions, untimed in practice (proposal's accessibility rule), pass mark 80%, with the final project still required. The exam covers interpretation (read a ledger page and predict a balance), production (record and post entries) and explanation, like the gates.

**Length and pass mark of every check [Raul Bautista, October 24, 2026]**: every quiz and test (module checks, Checkpoints A–C, placement tests, the prerequisite money check) is exactly 10 questions; placement is two stages, Modules 1–4 then Modules 5–8, each 10 questions **[Raul Bautista]**; the final exam is 50 questions. Every check passes at 80% of its points, with no other condition. **[Claude's call, approved by Raul Bautista]** Recording questions are worth more: a module check has 7 read-and-predict questions (2 points), 2 recording questions (4 points) and 1 explaining question (2 points), 24 points in all, so 80% is 20 points and a learner who scores 0 on both recording questions cannot pass. Partial credit on them still counts **[Raul Bautista]**: small slips on both can add up to a pass if everything else is right. No AI marks anything: numbers and entries are marked automatically and explanations are self-marked against a model answer, as before. **[Raul Bautista, October 24, 2026]** A recording entry with the wrong accounts on both sides (most often debits and credits reversed) scores 0, so a learner who reverses both recording questions cannot pass (16 of 24), while one reversed and everything else right still passes (20 of 24).

**Honest work and the certificate [Raul Bautista, October 24, 2026]**: The course opens with the author's note on honest work: no AI and no other people giving answers; there is no money reward and no benefit in cheating, so cheating only hurts the learner; "Earn your certificate." A Sonnet agent adapted the note from the author's own words for two places: the home page, before Module 1, and the final-exam page, just before the first question. Passing the final exam, with the final project marked complete, earns a **certificate of completion** that is unofficial and unaccredited and says so on its face. It shows the learner's name as they type it, the course title, the completion date and the credit line "Personal Bookkeeping 101 by Raul Bautista" with the CC BY-NC-SA notice. It can be printed and downloaded as a file. It is made in the browser, with no server and no accounts. The download format (for example a self-contained HTML or SVG file; the browser's print dialog can save a PDF) is chosen in Task 11.

**Estimated time [Claude's call]**: 40 hours main course + 7.5 hours add-on + 1.5 hours exam = about 49 hours, plus retakes (the proposal estimates 4 to 8 hours of retakes).

**Final project [Claude's call]**: one full month of books. Learners may use their own real month or a provided realistic sample month (for privacy, or for learners without income yet). The sample month is a fictional young adult with a part-time job.

## 5. Real-world figures and the inflation add-on [Raul Bautista]

- Every real-world figure (tax rates, contribution limits, company financials, drug prices, CPI) comes from a public, verifiable source, and the source is cited on the page where the figure appears.
- Corporate pricing stays in the add-on as a cause the author wants taught. It is taught with measurable numbers: simplified company ledgers built from published annual reports, from which learners compute profit margins before and during the pandemic.
- Graded questions cover only what has a clear factual answer (calculations from given figures, definitions, documented events). How much corporate pricing drove recent inflation is debated among economists; that part is presented with the evidence on each side, consistent with the course's logic-over-emotion theme, and is not a graded opinion question.
- Examples flagged in discussion as needing care: insulin list prices were cut by about 70% by the three main makers in 2023, and Medicare capped insulin at $35 a month from 2023; lisinopril and metformin are inexpensive generics. Examples must reflect current facts.
- The 2024 killing of the UnitedHealthcare CEO and the 2025 New York office-building shooting are excluded from the course entirely **[Raul Bautista]**.
- Dated figures (tax rates, limits) state their year so the course can be updated.
- **Paycheck examples [Raul Bautista].** The standard paycheck is two weeks of work at the **Arizona minimum wage of $15.15 an hour, 80 hours, so $1,212.00 gross**, with standard Arizona state and US federal deductions. It shows what a minimum-wage worker living independently actually has to budget with. The employer on the pay stub is fictional (a fast-food restaurant or car wash). **[Claude's call on the details]** Deductions use the 2026 figures: Social Security 6.2%, Medicare 1.45%, federal withholding from IRS Publication 15-T for 2026 (single filer, standard withholding), and Arizona withholding at the default rate an employee gets without choosing one on Form A-4. Every rate, including the $15.15 minimum wage, is checked against the official source (Industrial Commission of Arizona, IRS, Arizona Department of Revenue) before use and cited on the page.
- **Rounding [Raul Bautista].** Any amount that works out to a fraction of a cent (a tax or a percentage) is rounded to the cent by its thousandths of a dollar: "4 and below, keep it below; 5 and above, give it a shove." For example, 6.2% of $1,212.00 is $75.144, which rounds to $75.14. The course teaches this rule where rounding first appears (Module 4).
- **Fictional amounts [Claude's call].** Everyday amounts in fictional examples (a grocery bill, a rent payment) are invented and labelled "illustrative". Savings interest and credit-card APRs use sourced national averages for the stated year.
- **Choosing companies [Claude's call].** To avoid cherry-picking, the corporate-pricing lesson uses an objective rule: the five largest US drug companies and the five largest US health insurers by 2019 revenue, comparing their 2019 annual reports with 2021 and 2022. Counter-evidence (for example published studies finding cost pass-through) is also sourced and numeric. Insulin pricing appears as a documented case, including the 2023 price cuts and Medicare cap.

## 6. Learners at two speeds

- **Rereaders:** a "Reread this step" control on every step; supplementary practice sets built only from taught ideas; any module can be split across sittings; failed gates add that module's recall questions to the next three practice sets (proposal).
- **Fast readers:** an optional placement test built from Checkpoints A and B can unlock modules 5 or 9 (proposal).
- **Getting help [Raul Bautista].** There is no help forum. Each module ends with links to free articles on official sites covering its topics, such as the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, IRS, Arizona Department of Revenue, Federal Reserve, Bureau of Labor Statistics and Investor.gov. The proposal's "getting stuck" rule points learners to these links after rereading and reworking the worked example.
- Prerequisite self-check before module 1 **[Claude's call]**: basic arithmetic with decimals and percentages, with a short remedial unit for anyone who needs it (proposal's "learners who start behind").

## 7. The course website

**Delivery [Raul Bautista]**: an HTML course, Coursera-like in feel but with an entirely original design; public on GitHub; built in a local git repository first, pushed to GitHub later.

**Evaluation data [Claude's call].** The exported progress file records what the proposal's evaluation plan needs (time per module, attempts per gate, self-marked scores, checkpoint results, completion), so a pilot group can send in their files voluntarily. A four-week retention test page is included and unlocks 28 days after the final exam.

**Lesson page layout [Raul Bautista, approved from mockup]**: course map on the left (collapsible; finished modules ticked, current highlighted, later ones locked until the previous gate is passed); lesson text in the middle with step progress, "Reread this step" and a prediction box before every check; the current ledger page pinned on the right, with the line under discussion highlighted, and a print button. On narrow screens the map becomes a menu and the ledger is pinned at the top above the scrolling text.

**Technology [Claude's call]**
- A static site: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no framework, no build step, no runtime dependencies. One page per module plus home, placement, checkpoints, final exam and print pages.
- Hosted on GitHub Pages so anyone can open it with a link.
- **Saving [Raul Bautista]:** progress (completed steps, gate results, error log, the learner's own ledger entries) is saved automatically in the browser's local storage and must work in current versions of both **Firefox and Chrome**. It saves after every change, shows a small "Saved" indicator, and is restored when the learner returns, even after closing the browser. **[Claude's call]** It uses `localStorage` (supported by both browsers), the course must be opened from its web address (GitHub Pages or a local server), not by double-clicking the file, and export and import of a progress file let learners back up or move between devices. If storage is blocked (for example a private window), a banner says progress will not be kept.
- Ledger examples, exercises and gate items stored as data, so automated tests can prove every example balances and every running balance is right.
- Gate and checkpoint items are templates with varied amounts and names, so the proposal's four equivalent versions per gate (one per attempt) are generated rather than hand-written.
- Interpretation and production items are marked automatically; explanation items are self-marked against a model answer and rubric, as the proposal specifies.
- Accessibility per the proposal: real HTML tables for ledgers (screen-reader friendly), keyboard navigation, sufficient contrast, a large-text option, untimed gates, print styles.

**Licence [Raul Bautista]**: the whole repository, lessons and code, is under **Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)**: free for anyone to use, share and adapt for non-commercial purposes; no commercial use without permission; adaptations stay under the same terms; credit to Raul Bautista required. No "Copyright" line appears anywhere; every page footer reads: "*Personal Bookkeeping 101* by Raul Bautista is licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (free for non-commercial use)."

**Work split [Raul Bautista]**: Claude writes all code and drafts all content. Sonnet agents are used for English wording and prose polishing only. One Opus reviewer checks each major task when it is finished; there is no separate final review.
