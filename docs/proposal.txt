Proposal: A Standard for Self-Contained 40-Hour Courses
=======================================================
Length, structure, and teaching method: the systematic phonics model
Prepared by Raul Bautista - October 8, 2026
Revised October 24, 2026 (draft 3, phonics-led edition)

SUMMARY
=======

This proposal sets a standard for self-contained short courses: courses that
take a beginner from no knowledge of a subject to independent, durable use of
its core skills, without a live instructor. The standard length is 40 hours of
designed study, made up of 16 modules of 2.5 hours each, taken over about eight
weeks. Retakes of mastery gates add time beyond that; a typical learner should
expect 44 to 48 hours in all.

Each module pairs one hour of instruction with 90 minutes of structured
practice, so 40 percent of the course is instruction and 60 percent is
practice. Shorter courses can introduce a subject, but they rarely leave the
learner able to use it a month later.

The governing method is systematic phonics. Phonics teaches the underlying
code of reading explicitly, one correspondence at a time, in a fixed
sequence, and practices it until decoding is automatic. Every course built to
this standard teaches its subject the same way: the parts first, then how
they combine, in a fixed order, with practice material that uses only what
has been taught, until the learner can work out unfamiliar material from the
rules rather than guess at it. The supporting practices (worked examples,
retrieval practice, spaced review, and mastery gates) come from cognitive and
educational psychology and are chosen because they serve that method.

The standard rejects the three-cueing model, which teaches learners to guess
from context and patch over what they cannot decode. The guessing model looks
different in every subject, but its signature is the same: the learner
copies answers, leans on a tool, or changes an imitated example until the
result looks right, without being able to say why it is right. Studying a
worked example step by step is not guessing; it is the subject's equivalent
of being shown a letter and its sound.

The phonics model has a particular strength with learners who start behind.
The reading evidence shows it helping children at risk of reading difficulty
and older children with reading disabilities, and the author observed the
same pattern in classrooms over twelve years of schooling (see the section on
learners who start behind).

Seven commitments follow from this:

1. Teach the code explicitly and synthetically: the basic units first, then
   the rules for combining them, in a fixed sequence, with no more than two or
   three genuinely new ideas per module.
2. Use decodable material only: every example and exercise uses nothing the
   learner has not been taught.
3. Practice to automaticity: pair every hour of instruction with 90 minutes of
   structured practice, including short daily accuracy-then-speed drills, at a
   pace of about two modules a week.
4. Teach decoding before production: learners work through examples and
   predict their outcomes before producing their own work.
5. Gate progress on demonstrated mastery, checked against explicit rubrics and,
   wherever possible, an external check, so no learner advances on a
   foundation that is not there.
6. Allow no guessing strategies: no cues that let a learner reach the answer
   without decoding it.
7. Design for persistence: early wins, visible progress, a way back after a
   missed week, and a clear route to help.

The standard applies to any subject with a learnable code, meaning a set of
rules by which its symbols or parts produce their effects. Programming,
foreign languages, mathematics, music theory, bookkeeping, and electronics all
qualify, some with the subject variants described below. The appendix applies
the standard to a beginner Python course as a worked example.

THE PROBLEM WITH COMPRESSED COURSES
===================================

Most short self-teaching courses are sized by what fits into a weekend or a
handful of evenings, not by what a beginner needs to retain a skill. The
result is predictable. Learners finish able to recognize every idea they were
shown, but unable to produce working results without the lesson in front of
them.

Recognition and fluency are different achievements. Recognition means a
learner can follow an explanation and agree that it makes sense. Fluency means
the learner can use the skill unaided, at speed, on a problem they have not
seen before, and can still do so a month later.

Three features of compressed courses produce recognition without fluency:

- Too many new ideas per session. Most adults can actively hold only about
  four new, unchunked elements in working memory at once (Cowan, 2001). This
  is a limit on short-term capacity, not a measured learning rate, but a
  lesson that introduces eight or ten new elements leaves most of them
  unprocessed. The limit of two or three new ideas per module used below is a
  design inference from it, not a finding.
- Little or no practice. The learner studies examples but never moves from
  understanding one to producing one, which is where skill is actually built.
- Massed scheduling. Packing the material into a few long sittings feels
  efficient. It produces markedly worse long-term retention than the same
  hours spread out (Cepeda et al., 2006).

A fourth problem is hidden by the first three. When a course moves faster than
learners can decode its material, learners do not stop; they compensate. They
guess from context, imitate surface patterns, and lean on whatever tools will
fill the gap.

This compensation looks like progress until the material outgrows it. The
history of reading instruction offers a well-documented case of that failure.

THE PRECEDENT FROM READING INSTRUCTION
======================================

The clearest evidence on decoding versus guessing comes from a long argument
over how children should learn to read. Two families of method have competed
for most of a century.

Systematic phonics teaches the correspondence between letters and sounds
explicitly, in a fixed sequence. Children then blend those sounds to read
words they have never seen. Its strictest form, systematic synthetic phonics,
teaches individual letter sounds first and blending immediately after. The
research consensus behind phonics instruction is now commonly called the
science of reading.

Whole language, and its 1990s successor balanced literacy, treat reading as a
natural process absorbed through exposure to meaningful text. Kenneth Goodman,
the movement's leading theorist, titled his 1967 paper "Reading: A
Psycholinguistic Guessing Game." Balanced literacy added some phonics but kept
the central strategy of whole language, the three-cueing model.

Under three-cueing, a child who meets an unfamiliar word works it out from
three sources:

- Meaning: what would make sense here, often prompted by the picture.
- Structure: what would sound grammatically right.
- Visual information: usually just the first letter.

Sounding out the word comes last, if at all. The strategy succeeds on early
books with heavy pictures and predictable sentences. It fails around the third
or fourth grade, when texts outgrow their pictures and the guessing habit has
displaced decoding.

The evidence favors systematic phonics. The U.S. National Reading Panel (2000)
found that systematic phonics produced significantly better reading outcomes
than unsystematic phonics or none. Its meta-analysis of 38 experiments found a
moderate overall effect (d = 0.41) that persisted after instruction ended,
larger when phonics began in kindergarten or first grade (Ehri et al., 2001).
Phonics helped low- and middle-income readers, younger children at risk of
reading difficulty, and older children with reading disabilities; it did not
help one group of low-achieving readers that included students with cognitive
limitations. Synthetic phonics and other systematic programs produced a
similar advantage, and phonics outperformed every comparison method tested,
whole language included. England's Rose Review (2006) reached the same
conclusion and recommended replacing the "searchlights" model, a close
relative of three-cueing, with the Simple View of Reading. Synthetic phonics
then became national policy there.

More recently, Mississippi's 2013 Literacy-Based Promotion Act moved the
state's schools toward phonics-based instruction. Its fourth-grade NAEP
reading scores rose about 10 points between 2013 and 2019, the largest gain of
any state. The gain cannot be attributed to phonics alone: the same package
included teacher coaching and a third-grade reading gate that held back weaker
readers, which itself raises fourth-grade averages. The gain is consistent
with the phonics evidence but is not independent proof of it.

Two accounts explain why decoding works. The first is the Simple View of
Reading (Gough and Tunmer, 1986), which holds that reading comprehension is
the product of two separate skills:

    Reading comprehension = Decoding x Language comprehension

Because the relation is multiplicative, strength in one factor cannot make up
for weakness in the other. If decoding is near zero, so is comprehension,
however large the child's spoken vocabulary.

The second is orthographic mapping (Ehri, 2014). Each time a child decodes a
word accurately, its spelling, pronunciation, and meaning are bound together
in memory, until the word is recognized instantly. Guessing never triggers
this binding, because the child never fully processes the letters.

This standard adopts the phonics model as its governing method. The evidence
above comes from children learning to read, and its transfer to adults
learning other subjects has not been tested directly; the limitations and
evaluation sections say how the standard will test it. The adoption also
rests on the author's direct experience, described in the section on
learners who start behind.

THE PHONICS MODEL FOR ANY SUBJECT
=================================

Every subject this proposal covers has a code: a set of rules by which its
symbols or parts produce their effects. A learner who has decoded that code
can reason about material they have never seen; a learner who has only
learned to imitate surface patterns is confined to material that resembles
what they have already seen. Most subjects also need conceptual understanding
beyond rule-following, such as why a ledger must balance or what a function
is for, and the method treats that understanding as part of the code.

Each element of a phonics program has a direct counterpart in a course built
to this standard:

- Letters and sounds
    - In reading: Each letter or letter group and the sound it stands for
    - In any subject: The basic units and notation of the subject and what
      each one does (a value and its type, a word ending and the tense it
      marks, a debit and a credit)
- Blending
    - In reading: Combining sounds, left to right, to read a whole word
    - In any subject: Combining units by known rules to work out a larger
      result (tracing a program line by line, parsing a sentence word by word,
      posting a transaction to the ledger)
- Fixed scope and sequence
    - In reading: Correspondences taught in a set order, simplest and most
      common first
    - In any subject: Ideas taught in dependency order, each module using
      only earlier ones
- Decodable texts
    - In reading: Practice books containing only correspondences already
      taught
    - In any subject: Examples and exercises containing only ideas already
      taught
- Fluency practice
    - In reading: Repeated reading of decodable text until it is accurate and
      quick
    - In any subject: Short, frequent drills on core operations until they are
      accurate and then quick
- No cueing
    - In reading: No guessing from pictures, context, or the first letter
    - In any subject: No reaching answers by imitation, answer keys, or tools
      that do the work

Courses built to the standard follow four principles:

1. Teach the code of the subject explicitly, parts before wholes.
2. Teach it in a fixed sequence, where each step depends only on earlier ones.
3. Practice it until decoding becomes automatic.
4. Withhold the guessing crutches that let learners bypass decoding until they
   can decode and check what those crutches produce.

The two methods differ in the same ways whatever the subject:

- Core activity
    - Decoding model (adopted): Working out each step from known rules,
      including by studying worked examples step by step
    - Guessing model (rejected): Changing copied examples until the result
      looks right
- Unfamiliar material
    - Decoding model (adopted): Worked out from the rules
    - Guessing model (rejected): Matched to similar material seen before
- Source of answers
    - Decoding model (adopted): Produced by the learner from understood parts
    - Guessing model (rejected): Copied from examples, answer keys, or tools
- Response to errors
    - Decoding model (adopted): The error is diagnosed and its cause located
    - Guessing model (rejected): Changes are tried until the error disappears
- Where it breaks down
    - Decoding model (adopted): Less often; it scales as material gets harder
    - Guessing model (rejected): Once the material exceeds what can be
      pattern-matched

The guessing model takes recognizable forms in different subjects:

- In programming, copying code and changing it until it runs, or accepting
  whatever autocomplete or an AI tool suggests without being able to explain
  it.
- In a foreign language, machine-translating whole sentences, or recognizing
  familiar phrases without being able to parse an unfamiliar sentence.
- In mathematics, matching a problem to the worked example it most resembles
  and copying its steps without knowing why each step is valid.
- In bookkeeping, memorizing where entries usually go rather than knowing why
  every entry must balance.

In every subject, the equivalent of decoding is the learner's working model of
how the system behaves: how a program changes state as it runs, how a sentence
marks tense and agreement, how a ledger stays in balance. Programming
education calls this the notional machine (du Boulay, 1986; Sorva, 2013), and
the idea applies to every subject this proposal covers.

A course built on the decoding model makes that working model explicit from
the first lesson. Every new rule is introduced together with its effect, and
the learner practices predicting that effect before seeing it, just as a child
sounds out a word before being told what it says.

THE 40-HOUR STANDARD
====================

A self-contained beginner course should total 40 hours of designed study: 16
hours of instruction and 24 hours of structured practice, taken over about
eight weeks. Six to ten weeks is acceptable; the limits on pace set out below
matter more than the exact span.

The 40 hours are the course's length, not every learner's. With the target
first-attempt gate pass rate of 70 to 85 percent (see the evaluation plan),
retakes add roughly 4 to 8 hours across the course, and setup time adds a
little more. Course descriptions should state this plainly.

The benchmark is the university introductory course. Under the U.S. federal
definition of a credit hour (34 CFR 600.2), a three-credit course assumes
about 45 hours of instruction and 90 hours of outside work, roughly 135 hours
in all. A 40-hour course does not replace it and should not claim to; it aims
at the first level of independent use.

- Orientation
    - Total hours: 8-15
    - Instruction-to-practice split: Mostly instruction
    - Duration: 1-3 weeks
    - What the learner can do at the end: Recognize core ideas and follow
      examples; skills fade without further work
- Standard (this proposal)
    - Total hours: 40, plus retakes
    - Instruction-to-practice split: 40:60
    - Duration: About 8 weeks
    - What the learner can do at the end: Use the core skills unaided on new
      problems, and retain them months later (to be confirmed by the delayed
      retention check)
- Comprehensive
    - Total hours: 100-150
    - Instruction-to-practice split: About 33:67
    - Duration: 12-16 weeks
    - What the learner can do at the end: Work at the level of a university
      introductory course

Orientation courses have a legitimate use as a preview before a learner
commits to more. They should be labeled as such, and not presented as
producing competence.

What counts as an idea
----------------------

The sizing procedure below depends on counting ideas, so the unit must be
defined. An idea is one rule together with its observable effect: something
the learner can be asked to predict, apply, and explain. Closely related
forms of a single rule count as one idea (`if`, `elif`, and `else` are one
idea about choosing a branch). Every construct, symbol, or built-in that
appears in an example or task must either be counted as an idea in some
module or be explicitly listed as glossed material (see below). Course
authors should keep a "constructs used" list per module and check every
example and the final project against it.

Fitting a subject into 40 hours
-------------------------------

Because the length is fixed, the variable is scope. Of the 16 modules, 14
teach new material and 2 are reserved for a final project. With no more than
two or three genuinely new ideas per teaching module, a course can introduce
roughly 28 to 42 new ideas in total.

The procedure is:

1. List every idea the learner must be able to use unaided by the end.
2. Cut the list until it holds no more than about 42 ideas, keeping the ones
   that later ideas depend on.
3. Group the ideas into 14 teaching modules in dependency order, so that no
   module relies on an idea not yet taught. An idea that needs more than one
   hour of instruction takes two modules.
4. Reserve modules 15 and 16 for a final project that draws on everything
   before it.

The hours then follow directly:

    16 modules x (1 hour of instruction + 1.5 hours of practice) = 40 hours

A subject whose essential ideas will not fit within about 42 should be split
into two courses rather than compressed into one. Compression is exactly the
failure this proposal is meant to prevent.

Subject variants
----------------

The 16-module shape fits subjects whose difficulty lies mainly in a modest
number of rules. Some subjects also demand volume: hundreds of vocabulary
items, interval and chord recognition by ear, or many repetitions of routine
transactions. Such subjects may use the following variants without breaking
the standard:

- A volume track. Items learned by repetition rather than by rule, such as
  vocabulary or aural drills, run as a separate spaced-repetition track inside
  the 90-minute practice set. They are not counted in the idea budget, but the
  track's size and daily load must be stated in the course plan.
- Glossed material, kept to a minimum. In a foreign language, a short fixed
  list of essential phrases ("nice to meet you") may be taught as wholes
  before their grammar is decoded, the equivalent of the few irregular sight
  words a phonics program teaches early. They are marked as glossed, never
  used to stand in for decoding, and their parts are later taught as ideas.
- A different practice split. Where volume or receptive skill dominates, the
  instruction-to-practice split may shift toward practice (for example 30:70
  for a language), keeping the 40-hour total.
- Separate skill components. Where a subject has receptive and productive
  skills (listening and reading versus speaking and writing), gates assess
  each separately.
- A portfolio project. The final project may be a small set of pieces, such as
  three dialogues and a written text, instead of a single artifact.

Schedule and spacing
--------------------

Two modules a week is the standard pace, which completes the 16 modules in
eight weeks. Three a week, which finishes in about six weeks, is the maximum
for a beginner. Learners should complete no more than one new module per day,
and should aim for no more than four days between sessions.

These limits matter more than the total. Laboratory studies of spacing (Cepeda
et al., 2006; Cepeda et al., 2008) consistently find better retention when the
same study time is spread out than when it is massed, and there is no reason
to expect a 40-hour course compressed into two weeks to escape that pattern,
though the studies used shorter materials than a full course.

Adults with jobs and caring responsibilities will sometimes miss the four-day
limit. A missed week is not a reason to restart. The re-entry procedure is:

1. Redo the retrieval warm-up and exit check of the last completed module.
2. Reread the error log.
3. If most exit-check answers are correct, continue with the next module; if
   not, repeat that module's practice set before moving on.

On days when a full sitting is impossible, a minimum session of 30 to 40
minutes (one practice-set segment, or the next instruction segment) keeps the
spacing intact.

MODULE DESIGN STANDARD
======================

Every module follows the same 60-minute instructional structure, so the
learner's attention goes to the material rather than to working out what comes
next. The hour moves from recall, to guided instruction, to increasingly
independent work.

- Retrieval warm-up
    - Minutes: 5
    - What happens: Recall questions on earlier modules, answered from memory
      before anything new is shown (in module 1, questions on the course
      setup and orientation material)
- Explicit instruction
    - Minutes: 15
    - What happens: The new idea and its rules, then a worked example divided
      into labeled steps. The learner predicts the result of each step before
      it is revealed, and answers one self-explanation prompt
- Interpretation and prediction
    - Minutes: 10
    - What happens: The learner works through short examples, including at
      least one aimed at a known misconception, and predicts each outcome
      before checking it
- Guided practice
    - Minutes: 15
    - What happens: Faded examples, completion tasks, and modifications of
      working examples
- Independent task
    - Minutes: 10
    - What happens: One short problem solved from a blank page
- Exit check
    - Minutes: 5
    - What happens: Three to five ungraded questions confirming the module's
      core idea; the formal gate comes at the end of the practice set

The order is deliberate. Recall comes first because retrieving old material
strengthens it and prepares the ground for new material. Independent work
comes last, once the learner has seen, interpreted, and partly built the idea
with support.

The five-step teaching cycle described below maps onto these segments:
predict and check fall in the explicit-instruction and interpretation
segments, investigate in the interpretation segment, modify in guided
practice, and make in the independent task.

Cognitive load limits
---------------------

Each module introduces no more than two or three genuinely new ideas. Early
examples stay short enough to take in at a glance: a few lines of code, a
single sentence, one small diagram or ledger.

New notation and new concepts are introduced separately wherever possible, so
that a learner does not meet an unfamiliar symbol and an unfamiliar idea in
the same step. Cognitive load rises with the number of interacting elements
that must be held in working memory at once (Sweller, 2010), and a task that
consumes that capacity leaves little for learning (Sweller, 1988); keeping
notation and concepts apart is this standard's application of that
principle.

A continuous hour of new material exceeds what most beginners can process
well. The segment structure exists to break the hour into short stretches,
each ending with the learner doing something rather than only reading.
Self-paced learners should take a short break after the explicit-instruction
segment. The segment timings are guidance, not time limits.

INSTRUCTIONAL REQUIREMENTS
==========================

The following requirements apply to every module of every course. Each one
puts the decoding model into practice in a specific way, and each rests on
published evidence or follows directly from the model.

Explicit, synthetic sequence. Every idea is taught explicitly before it is
used, parts before wholes: the basic units and what each does first, then
the rules for combining them, as phonics teaches letter sounds before
blending. The sequence is fixed in advance and the same for every learner.

Decodable material only. No example or exercise contains anything not yet
taught, apart from material listed as glossed. Material a beginner cannot yet
decode, such as boilerplate code, idioms, or shortcut formulas, stays out of
examples until it has been covered, just as a phonics program keeps
undecodable words out of its practice books. A meta-analysis of experimental
comparisons found that explicit instruction outperforms unassisted discovery,
and that guided forms of discovery, supported by feedback, worked examples,
or prompts to explain,
outperform other forms of instruction (Alfieri et al., 2011). Kirschner,
Sweller and Clark (2006) argue that guidance matters most for novices. Their
critics accept that unguided discovery is weak, but point out that
problem-based and inquiry learning are heavily scaffolded rather than
unguided, and can be effective, including for school-age and lower-achieving
students (Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn, 2007). The standard is consistent
with both positions: whatever activity a module uses, the learner is never
left to discover a rule unaided.

Fluency drills. Decoding must become automatic, not merely possible, so that
attention is free for larger problems. Phonics programs build this with
repeated reading of decodable text, and the National Reading Panel (2000)
found that guided repeated reading improves reading fluency. Each practice
set therefore opens its interpretation work with a short drill on the core
operations taught so far (evaluating expressions, conjugating verbs, posting
entries), done first for accuracy and, once accurate, for speed.

Interpretation before production. Learners work through examples, stating what
each step does and why, before they produce their own work. The clearest
evidence comes from programming. Many first-year students cannot reliably
predict what short programs do (Lister et al., 2004), and scores on tracing
and code-explaining tasks together account for a substantial share of
code-writing scores (Lopez et al., 2008). These findings are correlational,
and a later replication suggests the link is looser than a strict hierarchy
would imply; they show that interpretation and production go together, not
that teaching one first causes the other.

Each subject has its own interpretation exercises: trace tables for code,
parsing and glossing for a foreign language, step-by-step justification for a
mathematical derivation, posting and balancing for a ledger.

Prediction before feedback. Learners commit to a prediction before checking
any answer, running any program, or revealing any solution. Checking without a
prediction is the equivalent of guessing from the picture. A wrong prediction,
by contrast, exposes the exact misunderstanding that needs correcting.

Worked examples that fade. Novices learn more from studying complete solutions
than from solving problems unaided, which is known as the worked-example
effect (Sweller and Cooper, 1985). Support is then withdrawn in stages: a
complete example, then one with gaps, then a blank page.

Fading matters because of the expertise reversal effect. Guidance that helps
beginners slows down learners who no longer need it (Kalyuga et al., 2003).

A fixed teaching cycle. Each new idea is taught in five steps: predict the
outcome of an example, check it, investigate how it works, modify it, and make
something new with it. The cycle is adapted from PRIMM (Predict, Run,
Investigate, Modify, Make), which was developed for programming. Early
evidence is encouraging but limited: in a mixed-methods study in secondary
schools, classes taught with PRIMM scored higher than a control group on a
post-test (Sentance, Waite and Kallia, 2019).

Labeled subgoals. Worked examples are divided into named steps, such as
"identify what is given," "choose the rule," "apply it," and "check the
result." Subgoal labels improve transfer to unfamiliar problems because
learners absorb the structure of a solution rather than its surface. The
effect has been shown with statistics problems (Catrambone, 1998) and in
programming (Margulieux, Guzdial and Catrambone, 2012).

Varied practice for transfer. The course promises that learners can solve
problems they have not seen, but far transfer, to contexts that differ from
the one in which something was learned, is uncommon and depends heavily on
conditions (Barnett and Ceci, 2002). Each idea is therefore practiced on
problems that differ in surface features (different topics, quantities, or
wording) while sharing the same underlying rule, and at least once per phase
the learner compares two worked examples of such problems and states what
they have in common. Comparing examples in this way helps learners extract the
shared structure (Gick and Holyoak, 1983).

Direct treatment of known misconceptions. Every subject has well-documented
beginner misconceptions. The course designer compiles them before writing any
module, and each module includes exercises aimed at the ones its content
invites, placed in the interpretation segment and the answer-key explanations.
Typical examples include:

- in programming, reading `=` as a statement of equality, which makes
  `x = x + 1` seem nonsensical;
- in physics, believing that heavier objects fall faster;
- in bookkeeping, assuming that a debit always means a decrease; and
- in a foreign language, assuming that word order works as it does in the
  learner's native language.

Errors as information. Error messages, wrong answers, and failed checks are
treated as information to be read, not noise. Before trying again, the learner
states what went wrong and why. In programming this means reading the error
message, which is taught from the first modules; in other subjects it means
finding the step where the work first departed from the rules.

No guessing crutches. The phonics model forbids cueing: a child is not taught
to guess a word from the picture, and a learner here is not given ways to
reach an answer without decoding it. In modules 1 to 8, learners make every
attempt themselves before consulting any tool or answer key. Tools that
produce the answer for the learner (AI assistants that write the solution, code
autocomplete, whole-sentence machine translation, answer keys) stay out of
reach until an attempt is complete. Reference tools that practitioners use and
that do not supply the answer (a dictionary, a calculator for bookkeeping, a
keyboard or tuner for music theory) are allowed from the start. Each course
publishes its own allowed and excluded lists.

Research on AI tools for novices is still developing and its findings are
mixed, so this rule rests on the decoding principle itself: a tool that
produces the answer lets the learner bypass the decoding the course exists to
build. From module 9, tools that practitioners routinely use may be used in
practice sets, because by then learners can check what the tools produce.
Gates, checkpoints, and the final project are always completed without
answer-producing tools, since they are where unaided skill is demonstrated.

No one enforces this rule in a self-contained course. Learners agree to it at
the start as a personal contract, and any exception is noted in the error log
with the reason, so the effect of exceptions is visible when they review their
results.

PRACTICE, SPACING, AND RETENTION
================================

Practice is where fluency is built, so it receives the larger share of course
time: 90 minutes per module, 24 hours in all. Practice sets are structured
rather than open-ended, and they rely on four techniques with evidence behind
them.

Retrieval practice. Recalling material from memory strengthens it more than
rereading it does (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). Every module opens with
recall questions and closes with an exit check, and every practice set
includes questions on earlier modules answered without notes.

Spacing. Review is scheduled at widening intervals rather than concentrated at
the end. Distributed practice consistently produces better long-term retention
than massed practice (Cepeda et al., 2006), and the best gap between reviews
grows with how long the material must be retained (Cepeda et al., 2008). A
workable starting schedule revisits each module's material after about one
day, one week, and three weeks. Each course includes a review map showing,
for every module, which later modules' recall questions draw on it.

Interleaving. Once learners know several ideas, practice sets mix problem
types, so the learner must decide which idea each problem needs. When every
problem in a set uses the idea just taught, the learner never practices that
decision (Rohrer and Taylor, 2007). Interleaving begins at module 5, once
learners have four modules of material to mix. Its evidence is promising but
less extensive than for retrieval and spacing.

Cumulative review. Recall questions and mixed production tasks together draw
20 to 30 percent of every practice set from earlier modules: the 10 minutes of
recall plus at least a third of the independent production tasks. In a review
of ten common learning techniques, Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated only two as
highly useful: practice testing and distributed practice. Cumulative review
combines both.

A standard 90-minute practice set runs as follows:

1. Recall questions on two or three earlier modules (10 minutes). In modules 1
   and 2, which have fewer earlier modules to draw on, the time goes to extra
   recall on the current module.
2. A fluency drill on core operations (5 minutes), then interpretation and
   prediction exercises (15 minutes).
3. Arrangement and completion tasks, in which the learner orders scrambled
   steps or completes a partial example (20 minutes). In programming these are
   called Parsons problems (Parsons and Haden, 2006).
4. Independent production from a blank page, mixing current and earlier ideas
   in varied contexts (25 minutes).
5. The module's mastery gate, followed by an entry in the error log
   (15 minutes).

In modules 4, 8, and 12, a 45-minute cumulative checkpoint replaces steps 3
and 4. The practice set may be split across two sittings, and spreading it
over a day or two strengthens the spacing effect. In subjects with a volume
track, 10 to 15 minutes of spaced-repetition review is taken from steps 2 and
3.

ASSESSMENT AND MASTERY GATES
============================

No learner advances to a new module until they have shown mastery of the
previous one. This is the self-teaching equivalent of a third-grade reading
gate: it catches a weak foundation before later material is built on top of
it.

Mastery learning, in which progress depends on meeting a set standard rather
than on time spent, was proposed by Bloom (1968). A meta-analysis of 108
controlled evaluations found that mastery learning programs improved
examination performance by about half a standard deviation on average (Kulik,
Kulik and Bangert-Drowns, 1990). The same analysis found larger effects on
locally developed tests than on standardized ones, a modest increase in time
spent on instruction, and, in self-paced college programs, lower completion
rates. A narrower review of group-based mastery programs in schools found
essentially no effect on standardized tests and moderate effects on
experimenter-made tests, and argued that the largest claimed gains came from
short studies that gave mastery classes extra time (Slavin, 1987). Mastery
gates are therefore used here alongside the persistence measures described
later, and the specific pass mark and gate format below are design choices,
not findings.

What a gate contains
--------------------

Each gate, together with the error-log entry that follows it, takes about 15
minutes and forms the last step of the module's practice set. It is scored out
of 10 points:

- Interpreting (4 points): work through two short examples and predict each
  outcome exactly; 2 points each, all or nothing.
- Producing (4 points): solve one short problem from a blank page using the
  module's ideas, scored against a four-item rubric.
- Explaining (2 points): state in one or two sentences why a given step works,
  scored against a model answer listing the required elements (for example,
  "names the rule" and "states its effect here").

Self-marking
------------

In a self-contained course the learner marks their own gates, and novices are
often poor judges of their own work. This is the main threat to the validity
of the gates, and every gate is built to reduce it:

- Every item has an explicit rubric of observable criteria and a model answer,
  so marking is a comparison rather than a judgment.
- Wherever the subject allows, the production item has an external check: the
  program runs and gives the stated output, the ledger balances, the circuit
  calculation matches a stated value, a recording is compared with native
  audio.
- Learners who have access to a study partner, tutor, or course community may
  have their answers to the explaining item reviewed by someone else.
- The evaluation plan compares gate scores with checkpoint and delayed
  retention scores, which shows whether self-marking is too lenient.

Passing and retaking
--------------------

The pass mark is 8 of 10 points, and the production item must score 4 of 4. A
learner who falls short follows a fixed sequence:

1. After a first failure, repeat the practice set with fresh problems, then
   retake the gate in a different version.
2. After a second failure, return to the module's worked example, rework it by
   hand, and write an error-log entry diagnosing what went wrong before the
   third attempt.
3. After a third failure, complete the module's short remediation set, which
   targets its most common misconceptions, and seek outside help using the
   rules in the section on learners without an instructor. Then retake the
   gate.

Every gate therefore needs at least four versions, one for each attempt in the
sequence. Versions count as equivalent when they test the same ideas, probe
the same misconceptions, and require the same number of steps. Authors should
build gates from item templates whose surface details (names, numbers, topics)
can be varied, and should check equivalence during the first trial run.

Retakes add time beyond the 40-hour standard, and this is intentional. The
standard fixes the length of the course, not of any one learner's path through
it; a learner who needs more practice should get it rather than be moved on.

Cumulative checkpoints
----------------------

Checkpoints follow modules 4, 8, and 12, taken after that module's own gate.
Each takes about 45 minutes and covers all material to that point, catching
material that passed its own gate but did not stick. The pass mark is 80
percent, each checkpoint has at least two versions, and every item is tagged
with the module it tests.

A learner who fails a checkpoint uses the item tags to find the earliest
module whose material they missed, and repeats that module's practice set and
gate, not merely the most recent one. That is where the gap began. Later
modules are repeated only if their items were also missed.

THE STANDARD COURSE TEMPLATE
============================

Every course built to this standard follows the same 16-module, eight-week
template, with the subject variants described above where needed. The subject
changes; the shape does not.

- Weeks 1-2: Foundations
    - Modules: 1 to 4
    - Purpose: The basic units and notation of the subject, and the working
      model of how they behave
    - Assessment at the end: Checkpoint A (after module 4)
- Weeks 3-4: Core operations
    - Modules: 5 to 8
    - Purpose: The main rules for combining the basic units, and the first
      interleaved practice
    - Assessment at the end: Checkpoint B (after module 8)
- Weeks 5-6: Extension
    - Modules: 9 to 12
    - Purpose: Larger structures built from the core, and breaking bigger
      problems into parts
    - Assessment at the end: Checkpoint C (after module 12)
- Weeks 7-8: Integration
    - Modules: 13 to 16
    - Purpose: The last new ideas in modules 13 and 14, then the final project
      in modules 15 and 16
    - Assessment at the end: Final project complete

Three rules run across the phases. The no-guessing-crutches rule applies
strictly through module 8, interleaved practice runs from module 5 to the
end, and decodable material and fluency drills run throughout.

Each phase also ends with a small real-use task: something the learner can do
with the subject outside the course, such as a short script that does a useful
job, introducing oneself in the foreign language, or recording a week of one's
own household spending as balanced entries. These tasks are short, ungraded,
and meant to make progress visible.

The final project
-----------------

The final project is a single substantial piece of work, or a small portfolio
in subjects that use that variant, completed from a blank page and drawing on
ideas from every phase. The learner chooses its topic within a specification
the course provides. Module 15 is spent planning it and dividing it into
parts; module 16 is spent producing, checking, and correcting it. Learners
should expect module 16 to run over its 2.5 hours, and may spread it over
several sittings.

Its form depends on the subject: a program of 60 to 100 lines, a short
conversation and written piece in a foreign language, a full set of accounts
for a small business, a working circuit with its calculations. What every
version shares is that the learner produces the result unaided and can explain
every part of it.

The project is assessed with a published rubric covering correctness,
structure, and explanation, plus a staged checklist (plan, first working
part, complete version, checked version). The explanation requirement is met
by a short written commentary in which the learner explains each part of the
work in their own words, checked against the rubric.

Outcomes
--------

By the end of a course built to this standard, a learner should be able to:

- interpret any example built from the course's core ideas and predict its
  outcome;
- produce correct work unaided, from a blank page, on problems they have not
  seen before;
- diagnose an error and locate its cause before changing anything; and
- break a larger problem into parts that use the course's ideas.

LEARNERS WHO START BEHIND
=========================

The strongest case for the phonics model is what it does for learners who
start behind. In the reading evidence, systematic phonics helped younger
children at risk of reading difficulty and older children with reading
disabilities, as well as low- and middle-income readers (Ehri et al., 2001).
It did not help every struggling group: one group of low-achieving readers
that included students with cognitive limitations did not benefit, so the
method is not a guarantee.

The author's own observation points the same way. As a student in the
Arizona public school system from kindergarten through twelfth grade, between
2006 and 2017, the author saw that teachers whose formal and informal
teaching leaned toward the older, systematic, explicit style (teaching the
parts of a subject directly, in order, with plenty of practice) had greater
overall success with their students than teachers who leaned toward newer,
less structured approaches. The difference was most visible among students
whose comprehension was below their grade level. Many other factors affect
outcomes, and this is a first-hand observation rather than a controlled
comparison, but it agrees with the published evidence and is a large part of
why this standard adopts the phonics model.

A course built to the standard responds to a struggling learner the way a
phonics program does: with more explicit instruction and more decodable
practice, never with a shortcut. In particular:

- Prerequisite check. Each course lists what it assumes (for example, basic
  arithmetic for bookkeeping, or comfortable reading in the course's
  language) and provides a short self-check with a remedial unit for anyone
  who needs it before module 1.
- Extra decodable practice. Every module has a supplementary practice set,
  built only from ideas already taught, for learners who want more before
  the gate.
- Smaller steps on request. A learner may split any module across two or
  three sittings and take the instruction segment twice.
- More frequent review. A learner who fails a gate adds the module's recall
  questions to each of the next three practice sets.
- No retreat to guessing. When a learner struggles, the course never offers
  hints that let them reach an answer without decoding it. The help is always
  a return to the rule and a worked example that applies it.

The evaluation plan reports results separately for learners who did and did
not need the prerequisite remedial unit, so the standard can check whether
the method serves learners who start behind as well as the reading evidence
and the author's observation suggest.

ADAPTATIONS FOR LEARNERS WITHOUT AN INSTRUCTOR
==============================================

A self-contained course has no teacher to notice confusion or keep the learner
going, so the course itself must do that work. The adaptations below take over
the functions an instructor would normally serve.

Feedback and diagnosis
----------------------

- Answer keys behind a deliberate step. Answers sit at the end of each
  practice set, not beside the questions, so the learner commits to a
  prediction before checking it.
- Explanations, not just answers. Every answer key entry explains why the
  answer is correct. For common wrong answers, it names the misconception that
  produced them. Feedback about the task and how to improve it helps more
  than grades or praise (Hattie and Timperley, 2007), and explanatory
  feedback generally helps more than a bare right-or-wrong mark, especially
  for novices, provided it is kept concise (Shute, 2008).
- Rubrics and checklists for open work. Blank-page tasks and explanations
  cannot be checked against a single answer, so each comes with a rubric of
  observable criteria and, where possible, an external check.
- A personal error log. Learners record each error, its cause, and the
  correction. Reviewing the log before each gate turns individual mistakes
  into targeted practice.
- Self-explanation prompts. Worked examples pause to ask the learner to
  explain a step in their own words before reading on. Learners who explain
  examples to themselves learn more from them, and prompting them to do so
  improves understanding (Chi et al., 1989; Chi et al., 1994).

Placement
---------

Adult beginners differ widely in what they already know. Each course offers an
optional placement test built from its first checkpoints. A learner who passes
Checkpoint A may start at module 5, and one who passes Checkpoint B may start
at module 9, avoiding the expertise reversal effect described above.

Persistence and pace
--------------------

Completion rates in open online courses are low and have not improved: in
2017-18 only about 3 percent of all participants in Harvard and MIT courses on
edX finished, though about half of those who paid for a certificate did (Reich
and Ruipérez-Valiente, 2019). Self-paced mastery programs have also been found
to reduce completion rates in college classes (Kulik, Kulik and
Bangert-Drowns, 1990). Motivation and persistence are stronger when learners'
needs for competence, autonomy (supported, for example, by meaningful choice),
and relatedness are met, though the evidence is weaker for relatedness than
for the other two (Ryan and Deci, 2000). The course builds these in:

- An early win. Module 1 ends with the learner producing something real,
  however small, from a blank page.
- Visible progress. A one-page progress map shows every module, gate, and
  checkpoint, and the learner marks each one off.
- Choice. The learner chooses the final project's topic and the contexts of
  the phase-end real-use tasks.
- Company. Learners are encouraged, though not required, to take the course
  with a study partner or a course community and to meet weekly to compare
  error logs.
- A fixed schedule. Learners choose specific session days at the start and
  treat them as appointments. Without an outside schedule, self-paced study
  tends to drift into the massed, irregular pattern the spacing research warns
  against.
- A realistic time check. If a module takes more than 4 hours, the learner
  checks whether they are skipping the worked examples or overdoing practice,
  and notes it in the log. Time overruns are normal, not a sign of failure. A
  sample 12-week calendar is provided alongside the 8-week one.
- A re-entry procedure after missed sessions, as described under schedule and
  spacing.

Getting stuck
-------------

A learner stuck for more than 20 minutes first rereads the relevant worked
example, then reworks it by hand, and only then seeks outside help. Each
course names where that help can be found: a course forum or community, a
study partner, or a tutor. Outside help is used to understand the problem,
never to obtain a finished answer; a useful request describes what the
learner tried and where they think the error lies.

These measures cannot fully replace a good teacher. They do, however, cover
the things a teacher contributes most often: catching a misunderstanding
early, keeping the learner at a steady pace, and keeping them going.

MATERIALS AND PRESENTATION STANDARDS
====================================

Course materials should direct the learner's attention to the content and to
nothing else. The standards below follow Mayer's principles of multimedia
learning (Mayer, 2020).

- Explanation beside the example. Commentary sits directly next to the step it
  explains, not in a separate block further down. Making learners look back
  and forth between an example and a distant explanation adds effort without
  adding understanding. Mayer's spatial contiguity principle addresses this;
  cognitive load researchers call the cost the split-attention effect.
- No decoration. Images, icons, and illustrations appear only when they
  explain something. Decorative material competes for attention, which Mayer's
  coherence principle warns against.
- Complete examples. Each worked example appears in full in one place, with
  its outcome directly beside or below it, and is never split across two
  pages. Where a subject uses code, it appears in a monospace font with line
  numbers.
- Meaningful labels. Names and labels describe what they stand for wherever
  the subject's own conventions allow.
- Signaling. Each module states its core idea in one sentence at the top and
  returns to it in the exit check.

Printed materials
-----------------

The standard print edition uses a compact layout to save paper and ink:

- body text in 10-point Times, headings in about 12.5-point bold Times, and
  any code in a monospace font at about 9 points;
- narrow spacing between sections and no forced page breaks, so sections flow
  continuously, except that a worked example is always kept together on one
  page (moved whole to the next page if needed);
- margins of about 0.85 inches at the sides and 0.6 inches at the top and
  bottom; and
- tables drawn with thin rules, with no solid header bars or shaded rows.

A large-print edition with body text of at least 14 points, wider line
spacing, and a sans-serif font is offered alongside it.

On screen, the same materials should discourage copying: learners write or
type out every example themselves rather than pasting it, in keeping with the
no-guessing-crutches rule.

Accessibility
-------------

- Every diagram, trace table, ledger, and piece of notation has a text
  alternative that a screen reader can present, or an equivalent described
  form.
- Audio material has transcripts, and video has captions.
- Text and background meet standard contrast guidelines on screen.
- Gates are untimed, and segment timings are guides only; learners who need
  more time take it.
- Writing out examples may be done by hand, by typing, by dictation, or by
  reading them aloud step by step, whichever the learner can do.
- Instructions use plain language, with subject terms defined when first
  used, to help learners working in a second language.

LIMITATIONS AND RISKS
=====================

The figures in this proposal are informed judgments, not measured constants.
No study establishes the exact number of hours that produces beginner fluency
in any subject, so the 40-hour standard should be revised against real learner
results.

The standard adopts the phonics model as its governing method, but the
decisive phonics trials were run on children learning to read, not on adults
learning a new subject, and the author's classroom observation, though
consistent with them, is not a controlled study. Whether the model carries
over to adult self-study in other subjects is what the evaluation plan must
show. Much of the other supporting research comes from individual fields, such
as programming, statistics, or algebra, and its transfer to other subjects is
assumed rather than demonstrated.

Several cited findings are also narrower than their reputations suggest. The
reading evidence is strongest for systematic phonics in general; whether
synthetic phonics outperforms other systematic approaches is still debated.
Mastery learning effects are moderate and smaller on standardized tests, and
the approach costs some extra time. Interleaving and arrangement tasks have
promising but less widely replicated support, so they are used here as
practice formats rather than relied on as proven effects.

The decoding model fits best where a subject has a clear code of rules. A
practical test is whether the core of the subject can be stated as a
manageable set of rules that predict outcomes. Programming, bookkeeping, and
the grammar of a language pass it; music theory passes for notation and
harmony but not for ear training. The model fits less well for skills that
are mainly physical, such as playing an instrument or a sport, and for
open-ended creative work. Courses in those areas can still use explicit
instruction, spaced practice, and mastery gates, but should apply the 40-hour
figure and the decoding framing with more caution.

Self-marked gates are the weakest link in the design. Rubrics, external
checks, and comparison with retention scores reduce the risk but do not
remove it.

Building a course to this standard is a substantial job. Four versions of
every gate, two of every checkpoint, retake practice sets, remediation sets,
rubrics, and fully explained answer keys can come to several hundred keyed
items per course. Item templates reduce the work, and authors should plan for
it from the start.

Learners vary widely in speed. Some will need twice the practice time allotted
here; the mastery gates are designed to absorb that, and such learners may
take twelve weeks rather than eight.

The no-guessing-crutches rule may meet resistance, since AI assistants and
similar tools are standard in many fields. The rule is temporary and has one
purpose: learners should adopt the tools only once they can decode what the
tools produce.

Finally, a longer course risks more dropouts than a short one. The persistence
measures above are meant to counter this, and completion rates should be
tracked from the first group of learners in every course.

EVALUATION PLAN
===============

Each new course should be treated as a draft until it has been tested on real
learners. Five measures should show whether it works.

- First-attempt gate pass rate
    - When it is measured: Every module
    - Target: 70 to 85 percent; lower suggests the module is overloaded,
      higher that it is too easy
- Time spent per module
    - When it is measured: Every module
    - Target: Median time within 25 percent of the 2.5-hour allotment. This
      measures the course, not the learner; individual learners may take much
      longer
- Delayed retention check
    - When it is measured: Four weeks after completion
    - Target: 70 percent or more on a cumulative test of interpretation and
      production
- Self-marking accuracy
    - When it is measured: At each checkpoint and the retention check
    - Target: Learners who passed a module's gate should mostly answer that
      module's checkpoint items correctly; a large gap suggests lenient
      self-marking
- Completion rate
    - When it is measured: End of course
    - Target: Recorded for every group and compared over time

Every measure is reported separately for learners who needed the
prerequisite remedial unit and those who did not.

The first group for each course should be small, about five to ten learners,
and its results treated qualitatively: with so few learners, one person moves
a pass rate by 10 to 20 points, so the percentage targets cannot yet be
judged. Each learner's error log and timings should be reviewed once that
learner finishes, and modules with low pass rates or long completion times
revised first, usually by splitting them or adding worked examples. The
percentage targets apply from groups of about 15 learners or more, or from
several small groups combined.

Before the template is declared general, it should be trialed on at least one
volume-heavy subject, such as a foreign language, as well as a rule-heavy one,
such as programming.

If most learners in a subject need substantially more than 40 hours, the
course's scope should be cut rather than its length extended. Of the five
measures, the delayed retention check matters most, because it tests the
outcome this proposal exists to secure: skill that is still there a month
later.

RECOMMENDATION
==============

Self-contained beginner courses should be built to a single standard: 40 hours
in 16 modules, each pairing one hour of instruction with 90 minutes of
practice, taken over about eight weeks. They should teach by the systematic
phonics model: the underlying code of the subject taught explicitly, parts
before wholes, in a fixed sequence, with decodable material only, and
practiced until it is automatic. They should exclude the guessing strategies
that three-cueing represents, and should answer a struggling learner with
more explicit instruction and more practice, never a shortcut.

Progress should be gated on mastery checked by explicit rubrics and external
checks where possible, and the course should be designed to keep self-taught
learners going, not only to teach them.

The same template, sizing procedure, and gating rules apply to any subject
with a learnable code, with the subject variants set out above. The appendix
shows them applied to one subject.

APPENDIX: WORKED EXAMPLE, A BEGINNER PYTHON COURSE
==================================================

This appendix applies the standard to an introduction to Python for complete
beginners. Its 14 teaching modules introduce 35 new ideas, within the limit of
about 42, and no module introduces more than three. Ideas are counted as
defined above; every construct the examples and final project use is listed
under some module.

Before module 1, a short setup guide (not counted in the 40 hours, about 30
minutes) covers installing Python and opening the interactive prompt and an
editor.

- Module 1: Values and types
    - New ideas: running code at the interactive prompt and from a file;
      numbers and strings; how Python evaluates an expression
    - Common errors targeted: treating `"5"` and `5` as the same thing
- Module 2: Variables and assignment
    - New ideas: names bound to values; reassignment; reading an error message
      (traceback)
    - Common errors targeted: reading `=` as equality; ignoring the last line
      of a traceback
- Module 3: Expressions and operators
    - New ideas: arithmetic and precedence; integer division and remainder;
      joining strings
    - Common errors targeted: confusing integer division with true division
- Module 4: Input and output
    - New ideas: `print()`; `input()`; type conversion
    - Common errors targeted: forgetting that `input()` returns a string
- Module 5: Conditionals
    - New ideas: comparisons and Boolean operators; choosing a branch with
      `if`, `elif`, and `else`; indentation as structure
    - Common errors targeted: confusing `=` with `==`; treating indentation as
      cosmetic
- Module 6: Loops I
    - New ideas: `while` loops; counters and stopping conditions
    - Common errors targeted: infinite loops; a loop variable that never
      changes
- Module 7: Loops II
    - New ideas: `for` loops with `range`; accumulating a result
    - Common errors targeted: off-by-one errors with `range`
- Module 8: Lists I
    - New ideas: creating and indexing lists; `len` and `append`
    - Common errors targeted: forgetting that indexes start at 0
- Module 9: Lists II
    - New ideas: iterating over and slicing lists and strings; changing lists
      in place, contrasted with strings, which cannot be changed
    - Common errors targeted: two names referring to one list; changing a list
      while looping over it
- Module 10: Functions I
    - New ideas: defining functions; parameters and return values
    - Common errors targeted: confusing `print` with `return`
- Module 11: Functions II
    - New ideas: scope; breaking a problem into functions
    - Common errors targeted: confusing local and global variables
- Module 12: Dictionaries
    - New ideas: keys and values; lookup and update, checking for a key with
      `in`; iterating with `.items()` and unpacking pairs
    - Common errors targeted: looking up a key that does not exist
- Module 13: String handling
    - New ideas: string methods, including `split` and `strip`; formatting
      output with f-strings; methods that return a new string
    - Common errors targeted: expecting string methods to change the original
- Module 14: Files
    - New ideas: reading text files with `with open(...)`; writing text files
    - Common errors targeted: forgetting the newline at the end of each line
- Module 15: Final project I
    - New ideas: none; the module is spent planning the program and dividing
      it into functions
    - Common errors targeted: writing everything at once before testing any of
      it
- Module 16: Final project II
    - New ideas: none; the module is spent building, testing, and debugging
      the program
    - Common errors targeted: changing code at random instead of reading the
      error message

Phase-end real-use tasks: a tip calculator (module 4), a number-guessing game
(module 8), a word counter for a pasted paragraph (module 12).

The final project is a program of 60 to 100 lines that reads data from a text
file, organizes it with lists and dictionaries, and reports results through
well-named functions. The learner chooses the data; a simple expense tracker
or gradebook fits. Its rubric checks that the program gives correct output on
a provided test file, that it is divided into functions, and that a short
commentary explains each function.

Tools: through module 8, AI assistants and code autocomplete stay switched
off; the official Python documentation is allowed throughout. From module 9,
practice sets may use autocomplete, and an AI assistant may be asked to
explain code the learner has already written, but not to write solutions.
Gates, checkpoints, and the final project are completed without either.
